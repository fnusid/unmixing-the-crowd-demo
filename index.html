Picking out each voice, no enrollment clip needed

Target speech extractors usually want a few seconds of someone talking on their own before they can pull that person out of a mix. In a real conversation you almost never have that. So we tried something simpler: take the speaker embeddings from the mixture itself and hand them to an off-the-shelf extractor that we didn't retrain at all.

Below are six recordings of two LibriSpeech speakers talking over each other (50–80% of the time), some with real-world background noise from WHAM! added. For each person you can flip between three versions. The extractor is the same in all three. The only thing we change is the embedding we give it.

Clean referenceThe person on their own, before we mixed anything. This is what we're hoping to get back.
OursOne embedding per person, pulled right out of the mixture. No enrollment clip at all.
Diarize-then-enrollThe obvious alternative. pyannote 3.1 finds the bits where each person talks alone, and the same encoder embeds those.

To be upfront, we picked these six from mixtures where ours does better. The average holds up too, though: across all 3,000 test mixtures, ours gets 10.8 dB SI-SDR improvement and diarize-then-enroll gets 6.8 dB.

How to listen. Hit play on one of the speakers and switch between Clean, Ours and Diarize-then-enroll while it's playing. It keeps going from the same spot, so you're comparing the exact same words. You can click anywhere on a spectrogram to jump there. Headphones help a lot, especially for the noisy ones.

About the numbers. They're SI-SDR improvement in dB, meaning how much closer the output gets to the clean recording compared with the raw mixture. Higher is better. To keep things fair, both methods share the same frozen extractor and the same speaker encoder, and both are told there are two people talking.

About the spectrograms. They show 0–8 kHz on a linear scale. Each one is scaled to its own loudest point and covers a 75 dB range.
